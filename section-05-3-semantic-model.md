# 5.3 The semantic model

The semantic model sits between the data warehouse and the reporting layer. Its purpose is to expose the analytical entities and the metrics of the domain in a form that reporting tools can consume without exposing the physical complexity of the underlying tables, and to guarantee that the same metric is defined in one place regardless of how many dashboards or reports refer to it. In the project analysed in this thesis, the semantic model is implemented across two technical layers: the gold layer in Snowflake, which materialises the fact and dimension tables in a governed physical structure, and the Power BI semantic model built on top of the gold layer, which defines relationships, calculated columns, and DAX measures.

The gold layer framework, including the naming conventions and the modelling patterns described in Section 5.3.1, was defined by the wider KPMG delivery team and applied consistently across all KPI domains of the project. The semantic model in Power BI for the student careers domain, together with the associated DAX measures, was designed and implemented by the author within this framework. The trade-off described in Section 5.3.3 emerged during the design of this model and was discussed and validated with the team.

## 5.3.1 Design principles and naming conventions

The gold layer adopts a dimensional modelling approach in the sense established by Kimball and Ross (2013): every fact table has an explicit and documented grain, every measure is aggregable along the dimensions related to the fact, and every dimension carries the descriptive attributes needed to slice the facts along the analytical axes required by the business.

The grain of each fact table is not left implicit. It is written into the header of the SQL definition together with the entity being counted or measured (for example, one row per student per subject per academic year), so that a reader of the code, or a subsequent maintainer, can reconstruct the intended level of aggregation without inferring it from the data. This is a governance choice at least as much as a technical one, because most of the errors that emerge downstream in dimensional models originate from a mismatch between the assumed and the actual grain of a fact.

Naming conventions play the same governance role. Every table in the gold layer follows a fixed pattern that identifies its role in the model: `TFCT_<PREFIX>_<NAME>` for fact tables, `TDIM_<PREFIX>_<NAME>` for dimension tables, and `VW_<NAME>` for views used to aggregate facts at a coarser grain than the underlying entities. Column names carry a two-part prefix that identifies the entity to which they belong and the type of the value they hold: `ID` for identifiers, `NR` for numeric values, `SD` for descriptive strings, `DT` for dates, `TS` for timestamps, `CD` for codes, `FL` for boolean flags, `PR` for percentages, and `HK` for hash keys used as foreign keys towards dimension tables. The convention is not decorative. It allows any reader of a query, including a reader who is not familiar with a particular table, to infer the semantics and the expected type of a column without opening its definition.

## 5.3.2 Structure of the gold layer

Fact tables in the gold layer contain the measures of interest for the reporting layer, together with the foreign keys towards the dimensions that describe them. Dimension tables contain the descriptive attributes of the entities that are shared across facts, such as the study programme, the student, or the academic year. Views are used when a report requires an aggregation across multiple facts at a grain that is coarser than the grain of the individual facts, without materialising an additional fact table.

Every foreign key in a fact is a hash key of type `BINARY`, computed as the MD5 of the business key of the corresponding dimension. This choice keeps the physical structure of the joins independent from the specific codes used in the source systems, and allows the same dimension to be referenced by facts that originate from different sources. To handle the case in which a fact record does not have a corresponding dimension record, the join expression uses a ghost record pattern: `COALESCE(dim.HK, MD5_BINARY('GHOST_RECORD'))`. A dedicated ghost record in the dimension collects all the fact rows whose dimensional attributes are unknown, which prevents unmatched joins from silently dropping rows and makes the incompleteness visible in the dashboards.

Dimensions are deduplicated at load time. Because a dimension aggregates multiple versions of the same entity coming from the silver layer, which is historised in SCD Type 2, the gold layer must select a single version of each business key. The pattern used is `QUALIFY ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY <business_key> ORDER BY <selection_criterion> DESC) = 1`, where the selection criterion depends on the entity: for a study programme, for example, the most recent activation date is used. The deduplication is performed in the gold layer rather than in the silver layer to preserve the historical detail in the silver, which remains available for point-in-time analyses when needed.

Joins that cross the source systems (mainly ESSE3, but also UGOV and other operational systems) are never performed on numeric identifiers, because different systems can assign identifiers in overlapping numeric ranges that do not refer to the same entity. The join is instead performed on the business code (for example, the code of a study programme), which is the only key that carries the same meaning across systems. Where a direct code-level join is not sufficient, the two systems are reconciled through a shared dimension in which both mappings are represented.

The physical refresh of the gold layer is managed through Snowflake Dynamic Tables, configured with a target lag of one day. The lag was chosen to match the refresh cadence of the upstream layers described in Section 5.2, and it aligns with the analytical needs of academic management, whose decisions on student careers do not require sub-daily latency.

## 5.3.3 The trade-off between shared dimensions and page-specific fact tables

The textbook shape of a dimensional model is a star schema in which one fact table is connected to a set of dimension tables, and in which dimensions are shared across facts whenever the same descriptive entity applies (Kimball and Ross, 2013). Shared dimensions are what allow a single filter, such as the academic year, to propagate consistently across all the facts that reference it, and they are the mechanism through which the model expresses conformity between different analytical subjects.

During the design of the semantic model for the student careers domain, this shape was adopted as the starting point. As the model grew to cover multiple report pages, however, a structural constraint emerged. When several fact tables share several dimensions, Power BI can find multiple paths between the same pair of tables in the relationship graph. The tool signals this situation as ambiguous relationships and either refuses to activate some of the relationships or forces one of them to be inactive, which breaks the intended filter propagation and produces incorrect or unpredictable results in the reports.

Two alternatives were considered to resolve the ambiguity. The first was to break the sharing and create dimension tables that were independent for each fact. This alternative was discarded because dimensions at different granularities cannot always be reconciled with the granularity of the facts that reference them: in practice, the same dimension used with different granularities produced repeated or incorrect values in the aggregated measures, which is a class of error that is difficult to detect visually in a dashboard and easy to propagate downstream. The second alternative, which was the one adopted, was to design page-specific fact tables that carry the descriptive attributes needed to serve one report page directly as columns of the fact, rather than through foreign keys towards shared dimensions. This design keeps the number of relationships in the semantic model small enough to avoid ambiguity in Power BI, and reduces the size of the model file by removing joins that are computed at query time.

The trade-off of this choice is that filters are not synchronised across all the pages of the report: because the same descriptive attribute (for example, the study programme) can appear as a column of two different fact tables that are not related to each other in the model, a filter applied to one page does not propagate to the other. This was discussed with the client, who confirmed that the lack of cross-page filter synchronisation was acceptable in the context of their intended use of the dashboards. The choice is therefore not presented in this thesis as a general recommendation, but as a documented trade-off between three competing requirements: the fidelity of the model to the ideal shared-dimension design, the ability of Power BI to activate the intended relationships without ambiguity, and the correctness of the aggregated measures at the grain of the individual reports.

## 5.3.4 Safeguards for data quality in the model

Several data quality safeguards are built directly into the definition of the gold tables, so that the model itself contributes to the quality of the numbers that reach the dashboards rather than delegating this responsibility entirely to downstream verification. Three patterns are used systematically and are worth mentioning at this level.

The first is the deduplication of dimensions through `QUALIFY ROW_NUMBER()`, already described in Section 5.3.2. Without this pattern, a dimension that inherits multiple SCD Type 2 versions from the silver layer would produce fan-out when joined to a fact, and every measure aggregated over that dimension would be inflated by the number of historical versions of the matching business key.

The second is the systematic use of `NULLIF` on the denominators of every ratio and percentage computed in the gold layer. This guarantees that a divide-by-zero situation returns a `NULL` rather than an error, which allows the reporting layer to render an empty value in the dashboard instead of interrupting the refresh of the visual.

The third is the ghost record pattern in the foreign keys, described in Section 5.3.2, which prevents unmatched joins from silently dropping fact rows and instead concentrates them under a dedicated ghost dimension row that is visible in the reports.

Beyond these embedded patterns, the design of a new fact table follows a verification step in which the row count of the fact is compared before and after each join with a dimension. A change in the row count that is not expected from the modelling logic signals a fan-out or a missing filter, and is treated as a blocker until it is resolved. Section 5.6 discusses the broader implications of these choices for the data quality of the reporting system as a whole.
